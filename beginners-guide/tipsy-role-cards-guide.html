What is a Role Card?

A Role Card (RC) is the persona you play when you chat — your name, appearance, pronouns, background, whatever you choose to include. Fill one in once, and Tipsy feeds it into every chat alongside the bot's own prompt, so the model already knows who it's talking to.

The question an RC answers is simple: when the bot looks at you, who is it seeing? Nobody supplies that answer except you — an RC exists so you don't have to re-explain yourself in every single conversation you start.

the key thing to understand Whoever built the bot you're chatting with has never seen your Role Card and doesn't know what's in it. They wrote their bot without you in mind at all. Your RC and their prompt only ever meet inside the actual conversation — never before it.
the part most people don't realise ❤

How your Role Card actually reaches a bot

There's no matching, no approval, no creator reviewing your card first. Here's what actually happens the moment you open a chat.

1

It's injected automatically

Every message the model reads includes your RC alongside the bot's own prompt. You don't paste it in yourself each time — once it's saved to your account, it's just there, every chat, every bot.

2

A well-built bot treats it as fact

Bots written carefully are told to treat your RC as ground truth about you and never invent conflicting details. That's what makes filling one in worth doing — it actually gets respected, not just skimmed.

3

No RC means the model assumes less, not more

If you haven't filled one in, most bots default to calling you "they/them" and avoid guessing at your appearance or backstory. Leaving it blank isn't a mistake — it just means the model has less to work with.

it shares the bot's memory budget Your Role Card sits in the same context window as the bot's own prompt — it isn't a separate allowance. The bigger and padded-out your RC is, the more of that shared space it takes up before the conversation itself even starts, which can mean less room for the bot to remember your last few messages.
the key distinction ❤

Your Role Card vs. the bot's character

It's not about length or detail. It's about which side of the conversation each one covers, and who's actually in control.

 The bot's characterYour Role Card
RepresentsThe AI-controlled chatbotThe persona you play
PurposeMakes the model consistently portray a character and run the experienceTells the bot who you're portraying
AgencyThe AI controls this characterYou control this character
IncludesBehaviour, voice, reasoning, setting knowledge, scenario rules, pacing, memory, trackers, boundariesIdentity, appearance, personality, background, abilities, behavioural tendencies, starting-state facts

The bot's prompt is a complete package its creator built — Name, Description, Opening, Instructions, Reply Settings, art. It answers: who is this character, how do they behave, what world are they in, and how should the roleplay run?

Your Role Card only ever answers one question: when the bot looks at you, who is it seeing? That's it. Nothing about the story belongs in it.

an RC isn't a way to become a bot If you want to be a full chatbot yourself — not just play a persona opposite one — that takes an entire character prompt of its own: Name, Description, Opening, Instructions. A Role Card only ever covers your side of someone else's conversation.
what you control ❤

What to include

An RC is a casting document. It's allowed to be detailed — on a general, reusable card, it stays out of the story (a dedicated card is the exception, covered further down).

❤ fair game for a Role Card
  • Name, age, gender and pronouns
  • Appearance
  • Role, occupation or social position
  • Personality, temperament and emotional habits
  • Strengths, flaws and vulnerabilities
  • Abilities or supernatural nature
  • Relevant background
  • Existing relationships or knowledge that are already true when a chat begins
  • Speech or behavioural tendencies, where useful
the power move ❤

Dedicating a card to one chat

Everything above describes a general Role Card — one persona you reuse across lots of different bots. Dedicate a card to a single ongoing story instead, and one of the rules loosens.

A dedicated card can carry plot. If it only ever gets attached to one chat, you can update it as the story moves — what's happened, what's changed, what your character now knows — and that history rides along with every message you send from then on.

it's your version of pinned memory This works the same way Tipsy's pinned memory feature does, just from your side of the conversation. If something important happens 40 messages back and it's about to scroll out of the bot's memory window, log it in your dedicated card instead of hoping the bot remembers — the card gets read in full every single message, so anything you put there stays in view.
the tradeoff is exactly why it works This only holds up because the card is dedicated. Take a card with logged plot on it and reuse it on a different bot, and that history either makes no sense in the new story or gets treated as already-established canon there too. One card, one story — and you have to update it yourself, deliberately. It doesn't log anything on its own.
the rule people miss ❤

The time rule

Your RC describes you at the exact instant a chat's Opening Message begins. Nothing after that instant belongs in it.

why this trips people up If an opening involves an injury, bargain, kidnapping, transformation, reunion, or revelation, your RC should only describe what was already true beforehand — or your neutral starting condition. Don't summarise what happens next in the RC itself; the model may treat that summary as completed canon and skip ahead entirely, before you've had a chance to actually play it out.

This is the default for a general, reusable card. If you've dedicated a card to a single ongoing story, that changes — see dedicating a card above.

writing your card ❤

Best practices

A Role Card works harder for you the more efficiently it's written. A few habits that make a real difference.

10,000 characters is plenty — if you stop writing prose Tipsy caps a Role Card at 10,000 characters. If that feels tight, the fix isn't more room — it's format. The info is there to feed the AI, not to read like a short story, and flowery prose eats the same memory budget as your actual facts for nothing in return.
❤ structured format — headers + bolded labels, no prose
## Rae Soma — The Gilded Heiress **Name:** Rae Soma **Age:** 28 **Gender:** Female **Height:** 156 cm …continues the same way for appearance, personality, background, and so on.

Markdown headers and bolded field labels pack the same information into a fraction of the characters a paragraph would take — and the model reads a labelled fact just as well as a sentence about it, usually better.

❤ a few more habits worth building
  • Spell out your pronouns. If you don't, the fallback is third-person they/them — fine, but worth knowing it's the default, not an assumption made about you specifically.
  • Write it so it stands alone. No creator has read your card and no bot was tailored around it — if a detail needs context to make sense, that context needs to be in the card too.
  • Don't contradict yourself. If two lines disagree about who you are, the model has to guess which one is true — update your card instead of leaving both.
  • Keep separate cards for very different personas. If you play a different kind of character across different genres or bots, one padded-out card trying to cover everything reads muddier than two clean, specific ones.
